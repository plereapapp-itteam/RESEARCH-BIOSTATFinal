Research process, study designs, sampling, literature search, reporting guidelines, ethics

Research Methods & Study Designs

TOS domain · Research

▪The Research Cycle (order matters)

Identify & delimit the problem→ Review the literature→ Objectives & hypothesis→ Methodology / design→ Data collection→ Analysis→ Interpretation & conclusions→ Dissemination
  • The research question comes first; it dictates what data are needed. Letting available data dictate the question risks collecting the wrong variables.
  • The literature review refines the problem, finds knowledge gaps, and identifies which variables (confounders, effect modifiers) deserve formal evaluation. It does not establish causation.
  • Methodology is fixed before data collection: design, participants, measurements, and analysis plan define how the hypothesis will be tested.
  • After conclusions are drawn, the cycle ends with dissemination (publication, presentation), which feeds the evidence base for the next cycle.

▪Sampling

Probability (random)
  • Simple random: every member has an equal chance; needs a complete sampling frame (roster).
  • Systematic: every k-th unit after a random start.
  • Stratified: divide into homogeneous strata, sample within each; ensures subgroup representation.
  • Cluster: randomly select whole groups; efficient for dispersed populations.
  • Multistage: clusters, then sub-clusters, then individuals (e.g., national surveys).
Non-probability
  • Convenience, purposive, quota, snowball, volunteer: faster, but prone to selection bias.
  • Consecutive enrollment of all eligible patients is the strongest non-random approach in clinical studies; better than physician-referred or volunteer samples.

▪Variables & Measurement

  • Independent (exposure, predictor) vs dependent (outcome).
  • Confounder: linked to exposure and outcome, not on the causal pathway.
  • Mediator: on the causal pathway (exposure → mediator → outcome); do not adjust it away.
  • Effect modifier: the association truly differs across its levels; report stratum-specific results.
Scales of measurement

RatioEqual intervals, true zeroWeight, height, BP

▪Research Ethics

  • Belmont principles: respect for persons (informed consent), beneficence, justice.
  • Declaration of Helsinki (WMA): ethics of research on human subjects.
  • Nuremberg Code: voluntary consent is absolutely essential.
  • Protocols need ethics review board (ERB/IRB) approval before enrollment; in the Philippines, the PHREB accredits ERBs and issues the National Ethical Guidelines.
  • Informed consent elements: purpose, procedures, risks, benefits, alternatives, confidentiality, voluntary participation, right to withdraw.

Descriptive statistics, distributions, confidence intervals, hypothesis testing, choice of test

Biostatistics & Hypothesis Testing

TOS domain · Biostatistics

▪Central Tendency & Dispersion

  • Mean is pulled by outliers; median is robust (use for skewed data); mode is the most frequent value.
  • Positive (right) skew: mean > median > mode (tail to the right). Negative skew: mean < median < mode.
  • Range, interquartile range (Q3 − Q1), variance, standard deviation.
  • Coefficient of variation = SD / mean × 100: compares spread across different units.

▪Normal Distribution

  • Mean = median = mode; symmetric bell curve.
  • 68% within ±1 SD, 95% within ±2 SD (exactly ±1.96), 99.7% within ±3 SD.
  • z-score = (x − mean) / SD: how many SDs a value lies from the mean.

▪Standard Error & Confidence Intervals

Standard error of the mean
SEM = SD / √n
Shrinks as sample size grows; SD does not.
95% confidence interval
mean ± 1.96 × SEM
99% CI uses 2.58 × SEM (wider).
Worked example
n = 100, mean = 80, SD = 10
SEM = 10/√100 = 1 → 95% CI = 80 ± 1.96 = 78.04 to 81.96
  • A CI for a difference that includes 0 is not significant; a CI for a ratio (RR, OR, HR) that includes 1 is not significant.
  • Narrow CI = precise, not necessarily valid. Bias shifts the whole interval.

▪Hypothesis Testing: the Error Matrix

H₀ actually true (no effect)H₀ actually false (real effect)
Reject H₀Type I error (α) — false positiveCorrect: power = 1 − β
Fail to reject H₀Correct (1 − α)Type II error (β) — false negative
  • α = P(reject H₀ | H₀ true), usually set at 0.05.
  • p-value = probability of results at least as extreme as observed, assuming H₀ is true. It is not the probability that H₀ is true.
  • Power (1 − β) = P(reject H₀ | H₀ false at the designed effect size); conventionally 80%.
  • Power rises with larger sample size, larger effect size, higher α, lower variability, and a one-tailed test.
  • An underpowered (small) study mostly risks Type II error: a real effect goes undetected.
Board trapType I and Type II errors are random (inferential) errors. Confounding and measurement bias are systematic errors; they are not what α and β describe.

▪Choosing the Statistical Test

SituationParametricNonparametric alternative
Compare 2 independent meansIndependent (unpaired) t-testMann-Whitney U (Wilcoxon rank-sum)
Compare 2 paired/related meansPaired t-testWilcoxon signed-rank
Compare ≥3 meansANOVAKruskal-Wallis
Two categorical variablesChi-square test; Fisher's exact if expected cell counts are small (<5)
Paired categorical dataMcNemar test
Linear relationship of 2 continuous variablesPearson rSpearman rho
Predict continuous outcomeLinear regression
Predict binary outcomeLogistic regression (gives adjusted OR)
Time-to-eventKaplan-Meier curve, log-rank test, Cox regression (hazard ratio)
  • Correlation coefficient r ranges from −1 to +1; r² = proportion of variance explained. Correlation is not causation.

Epidemiologic Measures of Association & Impact

TOS domain · Biostatistics / Epidemiology

▪The Exposure 2×2 Table

Outcome +Outcome −Total
Exposedaba + b
Unexposedcdc + d

▪Frequency Measures

Cumulative incidence (risk)
new cases / population at risk
Over a stated period; attack rate in outbreaks.
Incidence density (rate)
new cases / person-time at risk
Each person contributes time until event, loss, or study end.
Prevalence
existing cases / total population
P ≈ incidence × duration.
Case fatality rate
deaths from disease / cases × 100
Measures severity; not a true rate.
Person-time example
200 people, 3 years max
10 events at year 1.5, 20 lost at year 1, 170 complete → PT = 15 + 20 + 510 = 545 → 10/545 = 18.3 per 1,000 person-years
Board trapNew treatment that prolongs survival without cure raises prevalence. A shorter disease duration (rapid cure or rapid death) lowers prevalence even when incidence is unchanged.

▪Measures of Association

Relative risk (cohort, RCT)
[a/(a+b)] / [c/(c+d)]
RR = 1 no association; >1 harmful; <1 protective.
Odds ratio (case-control)
ad / bc
Approximates RR when the disease is rare.

▪Measures of Impact (exposure)

Attributable risk (risk difference)
Iexposed − Iunexposed
Excess risk in the exposed due to exposure.
Attributable fraction in exposed (AR%)
(Ie − Iu) / Ie = (RR − 1)/RR
Proportion of exposed cases preventable by removing exposure.
Population attributable risk
Itotal pop − Iunexposed
PAR% = PAR / Itotal; depends on exposure prevalence.

▪Measures of Treatment Effect

Absolute risk reduction
ARR = CER − EER
CER = control event rate; EER = experimental event rate.
Relative risk reduction
RRR = ARR / CER = 1 − RR
Looks large even when absolute benefit is tiny.
Number needed to treat
NNT = 1 / ARR
Always round up to the next whole patient.
Number needed to harm
NNH = 1 / ARI
ARI = event rate on drug − rate on control; round up.
Number needed to screen
NNS = 1 / ARR from screening
Persons screened to prevent one death.
Vaccine effectiveness
(ARU − ARV) / ARU × 100
= (1 − RR) × 100. ARU/ARV = attack rate unvaccinated/vaccinated.
Worked example (different numbers from the quiz)
CER = 20%, EER = 15%
ARR = 5% = 0.05 → NNT = 20; RRR = 0.05/0.20 = 25%; RR = 0.75

▪Standardization & the SMR

  • Direct standardization: apply each population's age-specific rates to one standard population; yields age-adjusted rates you can compare.
  • Indirect standardization: apply standard age-specific rates to the study population's age structure to get expected deaths.
  • SMR = observed deaths / expected deaths. SMR > 1 means more deaths than expected; < 1 fewer (e.g., healthy worker effect).
  • Expected deaths = Σ (number in each age group × standard rate for that group).

Diagnostic & Screening Tests

TOS domain · Biostatistics / EBM

▪The Diagnostic 2×2 Table

Sensitivity and specificity read down the columns (disease status); PPV and NPV read across the rows (test result).

▪Core Formulas

Sensitivity
TP / (TP + FN)
Among the diseased, how many test positive? SnNout: a negative on a sensitive test rules out.
Specificity
TN / (TN + FP)
Among the disease-free, how many test negative? SpPin: a positive on a specific test rules in.
Positive predictive value
TP / (TP + FP)
Among positives, how many are truly diseased?
Negative predictive value
TN / (TN + FN)
Among negatives, how many are truly disease-free?
Prevalence (in the study)
(TP + FN) / N
All with disease by the reference standard over all tested.
Accuracy
(TP + TN) / N
Overall proportion correctly classified.
Positive likelihood ratio
LR+ = sens / (1 − spec)
>10 strongly rules in.
Negative likelihood ratio
LR− = (1 − sens) / spec
<0.1 strongly rules out.

▪From Pretest to Post-test Probability

Pretest probability p→ Pretest odds = p / (1 − p)→ × LR→ Post-test odds→ Probability = odds / (1 + odds)
Worked example
p = 20%, LR+ = 5
Pretest odds = 0.25 → post-test odds = 1.25 → post-test probability = 1.25/2.25 ≈ 56%
  • The same LR applied to different pretest odds gives different post-test probabilities. Clinical context (pretest probability) always matters.

▪What Changes What

ChangeSensitivitySpecificityPPVNPV
Prevalence risesNo changeNo change↑↓
Prevalence fallsNo changeNo change↓↑
Lower the cutoff (call more positive)↑↓↓↑
Raise the cutoff↓↑↑↓
Board trapSensitivity, specificity, and LRs are intrinsic to the test (at a given cutoff). Predictive values are prevalence-dependent. Two populations with identical sens/spec but different PPV → look at prevalence first.
Board trapScreening a very low-prevalence population with moderate specificity produces many false positives: low PPV, extra confirmatory tests, referrals, cost, and anxiety. Targeting high-risk groups raises prevalence and therefore PPV.

▪Combining Tests

  • Parallel (positive if either is positive): ↑ sensitivity, ↓ specificity. Good when missing disease is costly.
  • Serial (positive only if both are positive): ↑ specificity, ↑ PPV. Screen with a sensitive test, confirm with a specific one.
  • ROC curve: sensitivity vs (1 − specificity) across cutoffs; area under the curve (AUC) 0.5 = useless, 1.0 = perfect.

▪Screening Biases & Criteria

  • Lead-time bias: earlier detection makes survival look longer without changing time of death.
  • Length-time bias: screening picks up slow-growing, less aggressive disease.
  • Overdiagnosis: detects disease that would never cause harm.
  • Judge screening by disease-specific mortality, not survival from diagnosis.
  • Wilson-Jungner: important problem, recognizable latent stage, acceptable test, effective treatment, cost-effective, continuous process.

Bias, Confounding & Causation

TOS domain · Research / EBM

▪Two Kinds of Error

Random errorSystematic error (bias)
AffectsPrecisionValidity (accuracy)
Fixed by larger sample?YesNo
Measured byp-values, CI widthCritical appraisal of design and conduct

▪Selection & Information Bias

BiasMechanismPrevention
Volunteer / self-selectionVolunteers differ systematically (healthier, more motivated)Random selection from a complete roster
Referral / physician-selectedReferred patients differ from all eligible patientsConsecutive enrollment of all eligible
Berkson's biasHospital-based controls have different exposure patternsCommunity controls
Healthy worker effectWorkers are healthier than the general populationCompare with other workers
Non-responseResponders differ from non-respondersFollow up non-responders; maximize response

▪Biases in Randomized Trials (risk-of-bias domains)

BiasArises fromSafeguard
Selection biasPredictable assignment (alternation, admission day, open lists)Random sequence + allocation concealment (central, sealed opaque envelopes)
Performance biasParticipants/clinicians know the treatment; care differsBlinding of participants and personnel
Detection (ascertainment) biasOutcome assessors know the allocation, especially with subjective outcomesBlinding of outcome assessors
Attrition biasDifferential, prognosis-related loss to follow-upComplete follow-up; ITT analysis
Reporting biasSelective outcome reporting within a published studyTrial registration; compare with protocol
Publication biasWhole studies with positive results more likely to be publishedSearch trial registries and grey literature; funnel plot
Board trapAllocation concealment (before assignment) prevents selection bias. Blinding (after assignment) prevents performance and detection bias. They are different safeguards.
Board trapIntention-to-treat analyzes everyone in the group they were randomized to and preserves randomization. Per-protocol drops non-adherent participants and can overestimate benefit when dropouts are related to adverse effects or poor response.

▪Confounding

  • A confounder is associated with the exposure, is an independent risk factor for the outcome, and is not on the causal pathway.
  • Classic: ice-cream sales ↔ drowning, confounded by hot weather; better-staffed hospitals ↔ higher mortality, confounded by severity of transferred patients.
  • Confounding by indication: sicker patients are more likely to receive the treatment, making the treatment look harmful.
  • A clue: the crude association weakens or disappears after adjustment for the third variable.
StageMethods to control confounding
DesignRandomization (known + unknown confounders), restriction, matching
AnalysisStratification, multivariable adjustment (regression), standardization, propensity scores
Board trapDoubling the sample size improves precision, not confounding. Blinding assessors addresses detection bias, not confounding. Effect modification is a real difference across strata to be reported, not a bias to be removed.

▪Bradford Hill Criteria for Causation

CriterionMeaning
TemporalityExposure precedes outcome; the only essential criterion
StrengthLarge RR/OR
ConsistencyReplicated across studies and settings
SpecificityOne exposure → one outcome (weakest criterion)
Biological gradientDose-response relationship
Plausibility / coherenceFits biology and known natural history
ExperimentRemoving exposure reduces outcome
AnalogySimilar exposures cause similar effects

Demography & Vital Statistics

TOS domain · Demography

▪The Denominator Decides the Measure

Crude birth rate
live births / total midyear pop × 1,000
Denominator: entire population.
General fertility rate
live births / women 15–49 × 1,000
Denominator: women of reproductive age.
Age-specific fertility rate
births to women aged x / women aged x × 1,000
One age stratum.
Total fertility rate
5 × Σ ASFR (per woman)
Using 5-year age groups; replacement ≈ 2.1.
Crude death rate
all deaths / total midyear pop × 1,000
All causes, whole population.
Cause-specific death rate
deaths from cause / total midyear pop × 100,000
Contrast with CFR (denominator = cases).
Infant mortality rate
deaths <1 yr / live births × 1,000
Denominator is live births, not the infant population.
Neonatal mortality rate
deaths <28 days / live births × 1,000
Post-neonatal = 28 days to <1 yr.
Perinatal mortality rate
(fetal deaths ≥28 wk + early neonatal <7 d) / (live births + fetal deaths) × 1,000
Fetal deaths enter both numerator and denominator.
Maternal mortality ratio
maternal deaths / live births × 100,000
The commonly reported “MMR”.
Maternal mortality rate
maternal deaths / women 15–49 × 100,000
A true rate; different denominator.
Rate of natural increase
CBR − CDR
Growth rate also adds net migration; doubling time ≈ 70 / growth %.
Worked example
Town: pop 50,000; women 15–49 = 12,500; 1,000 live births; 20 infant deaths
CBR = 20 per 1,000 · GFR = 80 per 1,000 women 15–49 · IMR = 20 per 1,000 live births
Board trapA maternal death is death during pregnancy or within 42 days of its end, from causes related to or aggravated by the pregnancy or its management, excluding accidental or incidental causes. “Pregnancy-related / pregnancy-associated” deaths count all causes and are not the same.
Board trapDistractor data (adults, total population, neonatal deaths) are often inserted into vital-rate problems. Identify the exact numerator and denominator before calculating.

▪Population Pyramids

Youngest cohorts at the base, oldest at the apex.
  • Expansive (broad base, narrow apex): high fertility, rapid growth, high youth dependency. Typical of developing populations.
  • Stationary (near-uniform widths, narrowing only at old ages): low, stable fertility with low mortality.
  • Constrictive (narrow base, wider middle): declining fertility, population aging, rising old-age dependency.
  • A bulge among middle-aged cohorts with a narrow base = an earlier baby boom followed by fertility decline.
  • A gradual shift from wide base to rectangular shape over decades = progression through the demographic transition.

▪Dependency & Sex Ratios

Total dependency ratio
(0–14 + 65+) / (15–64) × 100
Youth dependency ratio
(0–14) / (15–64) × 100
Old-age dependency ratio
(65+) / (15–64) × 100
Sex ratio
males / females × 100

▪Sources of Demographic Data

  • Census: complete count at a point in time. De jure = by usual residence; de facto = where found on census day. In the Philippines, run by the Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA).
  • Civil (vital) registration: continuous recording of births, deaths, marriages through the Local Civil Registrar, compiled by the PSA.
  • Sample surveys: e.g., National Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS).
  • Health information systems and registries: e.g., FHSIS for public health service data.

The EBM steps, PICO, hierarchy of evidence, GRADE, meta-analysis, statistical vs clinical significance

Evidence-Based Medicine

TOS domain · Evidence-Based Medicine

▪The Five Steps

Ask (PICO)→ Acquire evidence→ Appraise validity, results, applicability→ Apply with patient values & expertise→ Assess performance
  • PICO: Patient/Problem, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome (+ Time).
  • EBM integrates best research evidence, clinical expertise, and patient values and preferences.

▪Hierarchy of Evidence (therapy questions)

Higher tiers give stronger protection against bias; quality within a tier still matters.
  • Systematic reviews/meta-analyses of high-quality RCTs sit at the top for therapeutic questions.
  • Cohorts rank below RCTs because non-randomized groups may differ in prognosis despite adjustment (residual and unmeasured confounding).
  • Case series lack a comparison group. Expert opinion lacks systematic control for bias.
  • The best design depends on the question: prognosis → cohort; diagnosis → cross-sectional comparison with a reference standard; harm/etiology → cohort or case-control when RCTs are unethical.

▪GRADE: Certainty of Evidence

StartDowngrade forUpgrade for (observational)
RCTs start High; observational start LowRisk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, publication biasLarge effect, dose-response gradient, plausible confounding that would reduce the effect
  • Four levels: high, moderate, low, very low. Recommendations are strong or conditional (weak).

▪Meta-analysis Essentials

  • Forest plot: each study as a box (size = weight) with CI line; the diamond is the pooled estimate.
  • Heterogeneity: Cochran's Q; I² about 25% low, 50% moderate, 75% high.
  • Fixed-effect assumes one true effect; random-effects allows true effects to vary (wider CI).
  • Funnel plot asymmetry suggests publication bias or small-study effects.
Board trapPooling reduces random error but cannot remove systematic bias in the component studies. A meta-analysis can be precise and still biased (“garbage in, garbage out”).

▪Interpreting Results for Decisions

  • Statistical significance ≠ clinical importance. A tiny p-value with an NNT of 200 still means a small absolute benefit.
  • Relative effects (RRR) hide baseline risk; ARR and NNT translate the effect into absolute terms at the observed baseline risk.
  • For the same outcome, time frame, and safety profile, a lower NNT means greater benefit per patient treated.
  • Comparing NNT with NNH requires weighing severity, reversibility, baseline risk, patient preferences, and certainty; numbers alone do not decide.
  • Superiority trials test if a treatment is better; non-inferiority trials test if it is not worse by more than a set margin.

Quality Assurance & Patient Safety

TOS domain · Quality Assurance

▪Donabedian Model

Structure→ Process→ Outcome
ComponentDefinitionExamples
StructureResources and setting in which care is deliveredStaffing levels, equipment, facilities, qualified personnel, organizational capacity
ProcessWhat providers do in delivering careHand hygiene adherence, sterile technique, guideline-concordant treatment, waiting times
OutcomeChange in patients' health after careMortality, surgical-site infection rates, function, complications, patient-reported outcomes
Board trapOutcomes are the most direct measure of what happens to patients, but they are also the most influenced by case mix (baseline risk, comorbidity, behavior); they need risk adjustment. A comprehensive dashboard tracks structure, process, and outcome together, not satisfaction alone.

▪Dimensions of Quality (IOM, “STEEEP”)

  • Safe, Timely, Effective, Efficient, Equitable, Patient-centered.
  • Quality assurance checks performance against standards; continuous quality improvement (CQI/TQM) seeks ongoing, system-wide improvement.

▪PDSA Cycle (Deming / Shewhart)

StepAction
PlanState the objective, predict results, plan the small-scale test and data collection
DoCarry out the test; document problems and observations
StudyAnalyze the data; compare results with predictions
ActAdopt, adapt, or abandon the change; plan the next cycle
  • Small, rapid, iterative cycles; a shared organization-wide framework supports comparability and sustainability better than ad hoc departmental processes.

▪Quality & Safety Tools

ToolTimingWhat it doesWhat it does not do

▪Patient Safety Concepts

  • Swiss cheese model (Reason): an active failure reaches the patient only when holes in several layers of defense (latent conditions) line up. Fix the layers, not just the person.
  • Adverse event: harm caused by care. Near miss: error caught before reaching the patient. Sentinel event: unexpected death or serious harm; triggers RCA.
  • Errors of commission (doing the wrong thing) vs omission (failing to do the right thing).
  • Just culture: distinguishes human error, at-risk behavior, and reckless behavior; encourages reporting.
  • Forcing functions and standardization (e.g., patient-identification protocols, dose-range alerts) are stronger than education or awareness campaigns.

▪Accreditation, Audit & Monitoring

  • Licensing (DOH) sets minimum standards to operate; accreditation (e.g., PhilHealth, international bodies) uses externally validated standards for comparable benchmarking.
  • Clinical audit cycle: select topic → set standards → measure → compare → implement change → re-audit.
  • Continuous monitoring detects and corrects problems earlier than a single annual audit.
  • A structured RCA with targeted process changes produces more sustained improvement than an unstructured awareness campaign.

▪Final Board Distractor Pairs

  • Case-control starts from outcome (OR); cohort starts from exposure (RR).
  • Type I = false positive (α); Type II = false negative (β); power = 1 − β.
  • Precision improves with sample size; validity does not.
  • Sens/spec are fixed by the test; PPV/NPV move with prevalence.
  • Allocation concealment → selection bias; blinding → performance and detection bias.
  • Selective outcome reporting is within a published study; publication bias is about whole studies.
  • ITT preserves randomization; per-protocol may overestimate benefit.
  • Confounder distorts and is controlled; effect modifier is real and is reported.
  • Maternal mortality ratio uses live births; maternal mortality rate uses women 15–49.
  • IMR denominator is live births, not infants at midyear.
  • Structure = resources; process = actions; outcome = patient health change.
  • RCA is reactive; FMEA is proactive. Fishbone organizes; Five Whys digs deeper.
  • Statistical significance (p) ≠ clinical significance (ARR, NNT).

▪Integrated Program Design (Creation-level items)

Surveillance / define the problem→ Quantitative risk stratification→ Evidence-based intervention selection→ Iterative testing (PDSA)→ Predefined indicators feed redesign
  • The strongest framework is a closed loop: every stage feeds the next, and monitoring data drive redesign of later cycles.
  • Distractor frameworks usually skip one element: no formal risk analysis, no evidence appraisal, no iterative testing, or only “expand if trends improve” without predefined indicators.

▪Exam-Day Checklists

  • Any rate: What exactly is the numerator? The denominator? The multiplier (100, 1,000, 100,000)?
  • Any 2×2: Are rows exposure or test result? Are columns outcome or disease?
  • Any association: Chance? Bias? Confounding? Then consider causation (temporality first).
  • Any trial: Randomized? Concealed? Blinded (who)? Complete follow-up? ITT?
  • Any quality problem: Structure, process, or outcome? System cause, not individual blame.
