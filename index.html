Research Methods & Study Design

Design taxonomy, the research process, confounding, RCT integrity, sampling, reporting guidelines, program design

🧭Design Taxonomy — Identify It From the Starting Point

DesignWhat is randomized or selected firstSignature phrase in a stem
Cluster randomized trialIntact groups — communities, schools, clinics — rather than individuals"randomized 20 barangays to intervention or control"
Case-control studyParticipants selected by outcome status first, then prior exposure determined"cases with the disease and matched controls without it"
Cohort studyParticipants selected by exposure status first, then followed for outcomes"followed 1,600 exposed and 2,000 unexposed workers"
Controlled before-and-after / quasi-experimentalGroups compared across time without randomization"implemented in one province and compared with another"
Case seriesDescriptive only — no comparison group"we describe 14 consecutive patients who received..."
Board trapCluster randomization is defined by what is allocated, not by where the study happens. A community trial that allocates individuals is not a cluster randomized trial, and a study that allocates communities without randomizing is a controlled before-and-after or quasi-experimental design.

📚Literature, Databases & Reporting Guidelines

Databases
  • Embase — biomedical and pharmacological literature, indexed with the Emtree controlled vocabulary, with extensive drug coverage.
  • MEDLINE uses MeSH; Scopus and Web of Science are multidisciplinary citation databases rather than biomedical indexes.
Reporting guidelines
  • PRISMA-DTA — systematic reviews and meta-analyses of diagnostic test accuracy studies.
  • TRIPOD — development, validation or updating of multivariable prediction models for diagnosis or prognosis.
  • Baseline set: PRISMA for systematic reviews generally, CONSORT for randomized trials, STROBE for observational studies, STARD for individual diagnostic accuracy studies.

➡️The Research Process — Order Is the Answer

Problem→ Literature review→ Objectives & hypothesis→ Methodology→ Acquisition & analysis of observations→ Interpretation & conclusion→ Dissemination
  • Why methodology precedes data: it defines how participants, measurements and analyses will be used to evaluate the hypothesis. Design determines how data will be obtained, so it logically comes before collection and analysis.
  • Why the literature review matters: it provides the empirical and theoretical basis for deciding which variables and relationships warrant formal evaluation. It refines the problem and identifies gaps — it does not establish causation or substitute for primary data.
  • Why the question precedes the data: a predetermined question lets investigators identify exactly which observations are required. Letting available data dictate the question risks collecting irrelevant variables and missing essential ones.
  • Final step: after analysis, interpretation and conclusion, the remaining task is to communicate the findings through appropriate scientific dissemination channels.

🔀Confounding — Recognize It, Then Control It

  • Definition: a confounder is associated with both the exposure and the outcome and is not an intermediate step in the causal pathway.
  • DClassic stem: ice-cream sales correlate with drowning deaths → both rise with ambient temperature → the association attenuates after accounting for temperature → confounding, not causation.
  • DSecond classic stem: hospitals with more nurses appear to have higher mortality because sicker patients are preferentially transferred there → confounding by underlying illness severity.
StageMethodWhat it does
DesignRandomizationBalances measured and unmeasured prognostic factors — the only method that handles unmeasured confounding
DesignMatching, restrictionForces comparability on the matched variables
AnalysisStratificationEstimates the association within levels of the confounder
AnalysisMultivariable regressionAdjusts for the confounder while estimating the association
Board trapDoubling the sample size does not control confounding — it improves precision only. Blinding outcome assessors addresses measurement or detection bias, not confounding. When a stem asks which modifications address distortion by a third variable, the answer is stratification and multivariable adjustment.
Board trapEffect modification is not confounding. When the association genuinely differs across strata, it should be described and reported by stratum, not adjusted away.

🎲Randomized Trial Integrity — Five Named Threats

ThreatWhat happensDirection / consequenceSafeguard
Performance bias (no blinding)Knowing the allocation alters participant behavior, co-interventions or care deliveredEspecially damaging when the outcome is subjectiveBlind participants and caregivers where feasible
Detection biasAssessor expectation influences judgment of a subjective outcomeDistorts the measured effectBlinding of outcome assessors — the single strongest protection for subjective endpoints
Attrition bias40% loss in one arm versus 8% in the other, with poor responders leaving differentiallyDestroys the comparability randomization created; can exaggerate or attenuate the effectMinimize loss; intention-to-treat analysis; sensitivity analyses
Failed allocation concealmentRecruiters can anticipate the next assignmentPermits selective enrollment — a selection threat, distinct from blinding after allocationCentralized concealed allocation inaccessible to enrolling clinicians
Per-protocol-only reportingNon-adherent participants excluded, often those with adverse effects or poor responseTreatment benefit is typically overestimatedReport intention-to-treat as primary
Selective outcome reportingProtocol prespecified four outcomes; only the significant one is reportedDistortion within a published studyTrial registration and protocol comparison
DistinctionPublication bias concerns whether entire studies get published. Selective outcome reporting concerns which outcomes are reported within a study that was published. The stem that lists registered outcomes and shows only one reported is testing the second, not the first.

🎯Sampling, Enrollment & Internal Validity

  • Random selection from a complete roster beats volunteer self-enrollment, because volunteers differ systematically from non-volunteers and that undermines representativeness.
  • Consecutive enrollment of every eligible patient beats enrollment only of physician-referred patients, which introduces systematic selection.
  • Blinding outcome assessors is the strongest single protection when the primary outcome depends on subjective clinical judgment.
  • A wait-list control beats an uncontrolled pre-post design for internal validity, because it supplies the comparison needed to attribute change to the intervention.
  • Establishing exposure before outcome is the improvement that most strengthens causal interpretation in a cohort protocol — temporality cannot be fixed by a larger sample.

🏗️Creation-Level Items — The Closed-Loop Template

All five Creation items in this bank ask you to assemble a complete program, and all five have the same correct architecture. Learn the shape rather than the individual scenario.

1. Analyze surveillance data / define the problem→ 2. Stratify risk quantitatively (geography, age, unit, socioeconomic group)→ 3. Select evidence-based interventions→ 4. Test iteratively (PDSA cycles)→ 5. Feed monitoring results back into redesign
  • The correct option is the one where each component is deliberately linked to the next and the monitoring results feed back into future implementation. Options that stop after implementation, or that list activities without connecting them, are incomplete.
  • Scenarios used in this bank: adolescent vaping, dengue vector control, healthcare-associated infection, childhood stunting, road-traffic injury. The architecture does not change.

Biostatistics

Inferential error, alpha and power, risk measures, NNT and NNH, odds ratio, incidence density, SMR, confidence intervals

⚠️Inferential Error — The Four Definitions

TermDefinitionPlain reading
Type I errorRejecting a true null hypothesis — concluding an effect exists when it does notFalse positive conclusion
Type II errorFailing to reject a false null hypothesis — missing a genuine effectFalse negative conclusion
Alpha (α)The probability of rejecting the null hypothesis conditional on the null being trueThe Type I error rate you accept in advance
Power1 − β — the probability of correctly rejecting a false null hypothesisYour chance of detecting a real effect of the size you designed for
  • DSample size chain: smaller sample → lower power → a genuine effect is less likely to produce sufficient evidence against the null → higher probability of Type II error.
  • The question "assuming the alternative hypothesis is true at the effect size this study was designed for, what is the probability we will detect it?" is asking for power = 1 − β.
Board trapAlpha is defined conditional on the null being true. It is not the probability that the null is true, and it is not the probability that a significant result is a false positive in the real world.

🧮Risk Measures — Build the 2×2 First

Absolute risk reduction & NNT
ARR = CER − EER · NNT = 1 / ARR
Worked 70/700 control versus 42/700 experimental → CER 0.10, EER 0.06, ARR 0.04 → NNT = 25. Always round NNT and NNH upward to the next whole patient.
Absolute risk increase & NNH
ARI = risk(treated) − risk(control) · NNH = 1 / ARI
Worked 45/500 versus 15/500 → 9% − 3% = 6% → 1/0.06 = 16.7 → NNH = 17. Second example: 56/800 versus 32/800 → 3% → NNH = 34.
Number needed to screen
NNS = 1 / ARR, using mortality before and after
Worked 0.80% → 0.50% gives ARR 0.30% = 0.003 → NNS = 333.
Relative risk
RR = risk(exposed) / risk(unexposed)
Worked 72/300 = 0.24 versus 108/900 = 0.12 → RR = 2.00. Second: 96/320 = 0.30 versus 84/560 = 0.15 → RR = 2.00.
Odds ratio — case-control
OR = (a × d) / (b × c)
Worked cases 150 exposed / 100 unexposed, controls 120 exposed / 280 unexposed → (150×280)/(100×120) = 42,000/12,000 = 3.50. Read it as odds of exposure among cases versus controls.
Attributable risk & attributable fraction
AR = Rₑ − Rᵘ · AFₑ = (Rₑ − Rᵘ) / Rₑ
Worked 12% versus 6% → AR = 6%. And 24% versus 9% → 15/24 = 62.5% of disease among the exposed is attributable to the exposure, assuming causality.
Vaccine efficacy
VE = (Rᵘ − Rᵛ) / Rᵘ × 100
Worked 168/2,400 = 0.07 vaccinated versus 270/1,500 = 0.18 unvaccinated → (0.18 − 0.07)/0.18 = 61%. It is a relative risk reduction expressed as a percentage.
Incidence density
Cases / total person-time
Worked 40 cases at a mean of 1 year, 60 lost at 1 year, 300 completing 2 years → (40×1)+(60×1)+(300×2) = 700 person-years → 40/700 = 0.0571 → 57.1 per 1,000 person-years.
Standardized mortality ratio
SMR = observed deaths / expected deaths
Worked expected = (500×0.02) + (800×0.05) + (1,000×0.07) = 10 + 40 + 70 = 120; observed 84 → SMR = 0.70, meaning 30% fewer deaths than expected.
95% confidence interval for a mean
SEM = SD / √n · CI = mean ± 1.96 × SEM
Worked mean 118, SD 16, n 64 → SEM = 16/8 = 2 → 118 ± 3.92 = 114.1 to 121.9 mmHg.
Board trapTwo things break these items more often than the arithmetic: using the wrong denominator (person-time versus persons, exposed versus total), and rounding NNT or NNH down instead of up. You cannot treat 33.3 patients — you treat 34.

Evidence-Based Medicine

Evidence hierarchy, diagnostic accuracy, predictive values, likelihood ratios, screening, NNT versus NNH

🪜Hierarchy of Evidence — And Why Each Tier Sits Where It Does

TierDesignReason for its position
HighestSystematic review / meta-analysis of high-quality RCTsFor therapeutic questions, systematic synthesis of randomized evidence generally occupies the top tier
HighIndividual randomized controlled trialRandomization balances measured and unmeasured prognostic factors
MiddleProspective cohortNonrandomized groups may differ in prognostic characteristics despite sophisticated adjustment, including confounding by indication. Statistical adjustment cannot remove unmeasured confounding
LowCase seriesNo comparison group, so observed outcomes cannot be attributed to the intervention
LowestExpert opinionLacks the systematic control for bias built into structured study designs
Board trapA meta-analysis can be precise but biased. Pooling reduces random error by combining information, but it cannot correct systematic bias already present in the component studies. Narrow confidence intervals are not a guarantee of validity.

🎛️Diagnostic Accuracy — The 2×2 and Its Four Questions

Disease presentDisease absent
Test positiveTrue positive (TP)False positive (FP)
Test negativeFalse negative (FN)True negative (TN)
Sensitivity
TP / (TP + FN)
Among those who truly have the disease, how many test positive? Worked 216/240 = 90.0%.
Specificity
TN / (TN + FP)
Among the disease-free, how many test negative? Worked 680 disease-free with 34 false positives → 646/680 = 95.0%.
Positive predictive value
TP / (TP + FP)
Among those who tested positive, how many actually have the disease? Worked 216/250 = 86.4%.
Negative predictive value
TN / (TN + FN)
Worked 646/670 = 96.4%.
Prevalence
Existing cases / total tested
Worked 240 confirmed among 920 patients → 26.1%.
Positive likelihood ratio
LR+ = sensitivity / (1 − specificity)
Worked 0.88 / (1 − 0.75) = 3.52. Apply it to odds: pretest 0.30 → odds 0.429 → post-test odds 0.429 × 3.52 = 1.51 → post-test probability 1.51/2.51 ≈ 60%.
  • DThe central chain: sensitivity and specificity are intrinsic to the test → likelihood ratios are derived from them and are also intrinsic → predictive values depend on prevalence → the same test gives a different PPV in a different population.
  • Rising prevalence raises PPV because a larger share of positive results represents true disease. The mechanism, stated precisely: the ratio of diseased to nondiseased individuals entering the calculation has changed.
  • When two populations show nearly identical sensitivity, specificity and likelihood ratios but markedly different PPV, the component responsible is disease prevalence.
  • Converting probability to odds and back is what lets a single LR serve every patient: the LR is fixed, the pretest probability is not, so two patients with different pretest probabilities end at different post-test probabilities.

🔍Screening in Low-Prevalence Populations

  • The problem: screening for a disease affecting about 1 in 1,000 adults means the disease-free vastly outnumber the diseased. Even a modest false-positive proportion generates more false positives than true positives.
  • The consequence to name: increased false-positive referrals and confirmatory testing among disease-free individuals — cost, anxiety, and harm from downstream procedures.
  • The fix that does not require a better test: restriction to a higher-risk population. Targeting raises prevalence among those tested and therefore raises PPV, without changing sensitivity, specificity or the threshold.
Board trapPredictive value is not determined primarily by sensitivity and specificity, and PPV does not approximate specificity at high prevalence. Any option claiming PPV is prevalence-independent is wrong by construction.

⚖️Applying Numbers to Decisions — NNT, NNH and Clinical Significance

  • Lower NNT wins when outcomes and safety are comparable: NNT 8 beats NNT 40, and NNT 15 beats NNT 60, because fewer patients must be treated per outcome prevented.
  • NNT 25 against NNH 30 does not settle the question. These describe the frequency of benefit and harm, not their importance. The decision must weigh the clinical consequences of the two outcomes — severity, reversibility, baseline risks, patient preferences, follow-up period and certainty of the estimates.
  • Why report ARR and NNT alongside RRR: relative risk reduction describes the proportional effect but says nothing about how often the outcome occurs. Absolute measures incorporate baseline risk and translate the relative effect into expected clinical impact. They do not by themselves improve statistical validity or precision.
  • Dp < 0.001 with an NNT of 200: strong statistical evidence, small absolute benefit. A p-value measures evidence against the null, not the magnitude or importance of the effect. Clinical significance is judged from effect size, ARR, harms, costs, preferences and follow-up duration.

🏛️Donabedian — Structure, Process, Outcome

CategoryDefinitionExamples from this bank
StructureThe resources and setting within which care is delivered — the environment and organizational capacityStaffing levels, equipment, physical facilities, organizational capacity, equipped consultation rooms and appropriately qualified clinical personnel
ProcessWhat healthcare personnel actually do during the delivery of careAdherence to hand hygiene, sterile technique, surgical-site preparation, and to recommended clinical and preventive practices
OutcomeWhat ultimately happens to patients following care — a change in patient health30-day surgical-site infection rate; whether patients became healthier, survived, or regained function
  • The discriminating question: is this a resource, an action, or a consequence? An infection rate is a consequence even though infection prevention is a process.
  • Outcomes are the most direct measure of what happens to patients, but they are also influenced by patient and contextual factors outside the immediate care process — which is why the balanced dashboard reports all three.
Board trapGuideline availability is structure; guideline adherence is process; the infection rate that follows is outcome. The same subject matter can appear in all three categories, so classify by the noun, not the topic.

🔁PDSA — and the Step Teams Skip

Plan — define the change and the measure→ Do — implement on a small scale→ Study — analyze whether it produced improvement→ Act — adopt, adapt or abandon
  • A team that repeatedly implements changes but never formally analyzes whether they worked has skipped the Study step. That is the single most commonly omitted stage and the one this bank tests.
  • A validated framework applied organization-wide beats letting each department define its own ad hoc process, because it supports consistency, comparability and sustainability.

🐟Incident Analysis — Three Tools, Three Jobs

Root cause analysis
Adverse event → interacting system-level vulnerabilities
Treats an event as the product of interacting system failures rather than assigning it to a single proximal cause or individual. A wrong-patient medication error traced to a missing standardized identification protocol at multiple workflow points is a systems-level root cause, not individual blame.
Fishbone (Ishikawa) diagram
Organizes candidate causes into system domains
Sorts contributors such as interruptions under Environment, barcode inconsistency under Process, similar packaging under Equipment. It facilitates exploration but does not by itself determine which cause is responsible, and it quantifies nothing.
Five Whys
Iterative causal questioning
Moves from an immediately visible failure — "the nurse did not notice the electronic order" — toward the deeper processes and system conditions that permitted it. It traces proximal failures to system conditions; it does not quantify or rank them.
  • DActive error plus latent conditions: a tenfold overdose entered by a prescriber, passing through an absent dose-range alert and an unchecked verification step, is multiple system vulnerabilities aligning with an active error so the error reached the patient. The prescribing error alone is not the root cause.
  • Inadequate staffing during peak hours, absent hourly rounding and an unaddressed environmental hazard preceding a fall are interacting system failures, not a single cause.

🎯One-Line Pairings

Design and methods
  • Randomizes intact groups → cluster randomized trial.
  • Starts from outcome status, looks back at exposure → case-control.
  • Emtree indexing, pharmacological coverage → Embase.
  • Systematic review of diagnostic accuracy → PRISMA-DTA. Prediction model development or validation → TRIPOD.
  • Methodology precedes data; the question precedes the data; dissemination is last.
  • Associated with both exposure and outcome, not on the causal pathway → confounder.
  • Controls confounding → randomization, matching, restriction, stratification, multivariable adjustment. Does NOT → bigger sample, assessor blinding.
  • Subjective outcome, unblinded → performance and detection bias. Blind the assessors.
  • 40% versus 8% dropout, prognosis-related → attrition bias destroys randomization's balance.
  • Recruiter can predict the next assignment → failed allocation concealment → selective enrollment.
  • Per-protocol only → benefit overestimated. Report intention-to-treat.
  • Four registered outcomes, one reported → selective outcome reporting, not publication bias.
  • Volunteers versus roster → random selection. Referrals versus everyone eligible → consecutive enrollment.
  • Exposure must be established before outcome → temporality; a bigger sample cannot fix it.
Biostatistics
  • Rejecting a true null → Type I. Failing to reject a false null → Type II.
  • Alpha = probability of rejecting the null given the null is true. Power = 1 − β.
  • Small sample → low power → more Type II error.
  • NNT = 1/ARR, NNH = 1/ARI, NNS = 1/ARR — always round up.
  • Case-control → odds ratio (a×d)/(b×c). Cohort → relative risk.
  • Person-time denominator → incidence density. Observed over expected → SMR.
  • SEM = SD/√n; 95% CI = mean ± 1.96 × SEM.
Demography
  • Denominator = total midyear population → CBR and CDR. Denominator = women 15–49 → GFR. Denominator = live births → IMR, NMR, MMR.
  • Only the MMR is per 100,000.
  • Maternal deaths ≠ pregnancy-associated deaths.
  • Broad base → high fertility and high youth dependency. Rectangular → demographic transition toward lower fertility. Narrow base with a bulge → fertility decline after a boom.
EBM and quality assurance
  • Top of the hierarchy for therapy → systematic review of high-quality RCTs. Bottom → expert opinion, because it lacks systematic bias control.
  • Cohort sits below RCT because unmeasured prognostic differences survive statistical adjustment.
  • Meta-analysis → precise but potentially biased; pooling fixes random error, not systematic bias.
  • Sensitivity and specificity are intrinsic. Predictive values move with prevalence.
  • Low prevalence → false positives dominate → restrict to a higher-risk group to raise PPV.
  • LR multiplies odds, not probability.
  • Lower NNT wins; NNT versus NNH needs the clinical weight of each outcome, not just the numbers.
  • Tiny p-value with NNT 200 → statistically strong, clinically small.
  • Resources → structure. Actions → process. Patient consequences → outcome.
  • Implemented but never analyzed → skipped the Study step of PDSA.
  • RCA finds interacting system vulnerabilities; fishbone organizes candidates without ranking them; Five Whys traces proximal failure to system conditions.
  • Structured beats unstructured, validated beats ad hoc, continuous beats annual, multidimensional beats single-metric.

🚫Distractor Patterns This Bank Reuses

  • Denominator swaps in every demographic rate item — the stem deliberately supplies the decoy denominator.
  • Precision offered as a cure for bias: bigger sample, narrower confidence interval, or pooling described as fixing confounding or systematic error.
  • Predictive value described as prevalence-independent, or PPV said to approximate specificity.
  • Statistical significance equated with clinical importance, usually via a very small p-value attached to a large NNT.
  • Individual blame offered in place of a systems root cause in every incident-analysis item.
  • A single number treated as a decision — NNT versus NNH compared numerically without weighing outcome severity.
  • Effect modification labeled as confounding, with an option suggesting it be adjusted away.
  • Incomplete program architectures in the Creation items, which stop at implementation instead of feeding monitoring back into redesign.
